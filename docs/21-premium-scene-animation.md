# Premium Scene Animation — Skeptical Code & Visual QA Review

**Scope:** Kumiki UI PR #45, 26 recipes, shared ST1 renderer, FS1 and PJ1, Storybook gallery, 26-video export pipeline. **Review method:** source/API/test inspection. **Not performed:** interactive browser screenshots, normal-speed footage review, listening to audio, and proof of physical 3D realism. This is not a visual signoff.

## Finding summary

**P1 — Real visual qualification remains absent.** Having a typed component and passing DOM tests does not prove that a rendered clip looks cinematic. The current GitHub PR has no verified full 26-scene video output artifact on its latest commits. Keep draft. Require source Storybook clips plus an annotated visual review of first, transformation, and last frames in both aspects. Verify at normal playback speed, not just screenshot poses.

**P1 — Source illustrations were misleadingly repetitive.** The initial Storybook fixture rendered the same mountain motif for almost every scene, concealing whether globe, book, UI, device, evidence and architectural scenes worked. Fixed in the branch with distinct original fixture motifs (house, network, UI, document, exploded parts, planet, character, craft, office). These SVGs remain QA art only, not premium stock footage.

**P1 — Editorial camera direction was reversed.** The original generic cinematic helper began zoomed in and pulled out during a claimed push-in. Fixed with frame-exact zoom **toward** the subject, a clamped camera translation to prevent blank edges, and regression assertions.

**P2 — Scene-specific visual quality gaps remain.** CSS transforms, 2.5D layering, and basic primitive drawings are useful fallbacks, not substitutes for realistic articulated character animation, 3D earth geometry, mesh cutaways, glass refraction, or curved-page simulation. The optional host renderer slots can integrate these with existing Kumiki renderers; they do not themselves implement those physical effects.

**P2 — User-content robustness is not browser-qualified.** 9:16, long paragraphs, CJK text, many timeline points, variable aspect-ratio imagery, reduced motion, region target registration and caption-safe areas must be tested using realistic authored content. Current tests check semantic state, DOM presence and basic geometry, but not visual desirability.

## Creative direction: production targets for the existing 26 recipes

These are **proposed directions**, not claims about what the current implementation renders. The review table below records the inspected implementation and its unresolved gates. Each direction is a reusable shot grammar: swap the subject, environment, media and editorial language without baking a particular industry or story into the component. Choose a camera move because it reveals information; a locked shot, a motivated cut and silence can be more cinematic than constant movement.

A premium production begins with an authored shot list, visual references, real scale, lighting continuity, lens and depth-of-field choices, color grade, precise sound and a deliberate hold on the payoff. Separate narration, music and effects on a common frame timeline; leave room for voice and license every track. Text is measured in its actual font and output aspect, with protected areas for captions, source credits and moving subjects. Explainers retain one observable input → transformation → output per beat. A scene must also work in a reduced-motion edit. Native 3D, footage, compositing, simulation and sound are production dependencies where described—not capabilities implied by the current 2D fallback. Apply the [visual production contract](visual-production-contract.md), [3D fidelity gate](14-3d-visual-fidelity.md) and [asset sourcing rules](ASSET_SOURCING.md).

1. **`question-reveal` — The question as a dramatic premise.** Open on a tangible subject in an environment that makes the question consequential: a machine paused mid-operation, a landscape about to change, or a person waiting for an outcome. Hold a composed wide shot, then make one restrained move toward the decisive detail; introduce the question in a protected editorial area. Answer through an observable change in the subject, then hold the answer and its visual evidence together. Use practical motivated light and a quiet sound cue rather than a title animation doing all the work. Requires a subject/action and a legible answer state.
2. **`hidden-detail` — The clue inside the frame.** Establish the whole object before guiding the eye to the small feature that changes its interpretation. A measured push or cut to a matched macro angle reveals surface texture and the feature in context; a subtle highlight or annotation appears only after the viewer has found it. Match lens, lighting and spatial registration across the cut, and retain a small source/context view if the close-up loses orientation. Requires a real high-resolution target or close-up asset; a scanning beam alone is not discovery.
3. **`process-journey` — Follow the thing that changes.** Start with an identifiable input in a spatially coherent environment, follow it through each operation and finish on the altered output. Use a stable master shot with selective motivated cuts or tracking at handoffs; maintain subject identity, actual connection points and direction of travel. Material response and sound distinguish each operation, while labels stay in a fixed safe lane. Requires authored routes, semantic transfer events and an explicit result.
4. **`before-after` — A controlled transformation.** Show the original in a composed locked frame; reveal the change through a motivated boundary or a matched cut, then hold the result long enough to compare. Keep camera position, focal length, scale, lighting and crop registered unless the changed condition itself affects them. For physical change, use matching geometry or footage and credible material continuity. Declare what changed and preserve source context; a flashy wipe cannot repair mismatched images.
5. **`side-by-side` — An editorial comparison.** Stage two subjects as if photographed for the same program: matching camera angle, scale, horizon, exposure and a shared baseline. Establish the comparison criterion, then reveal the relevant difference simultaneously or in a clear sequence; use a quiet divider and a concluding visual emphasis. A split screen can become stacked compositions in portrait without changing the comparison. Requires comparable source assets and explicit units or conditions where relevant.
6. **`cause-effect` — Make the mechanism visible.** Begin with a single physical or semantic cause, show the transfer through an attached connection or material reaction, and land on the effect in the same visual world. Use a locked or gently reframed shot until a cut clarifies the next stage; emphasize the active link rather than filling the screen with tiles. Distinguish a demonstrated mechanism from a merely correlated sequence. Requires causal structure supplied by the author and a visible end state.
7. **`scale-reveal` — Reveal magnitude through a common world.** Establish a known reference, place the second subject on the same scale, then widen or cut to a calibrated comparison that exposes the difference. For extreme ratios use an explicitly labeled scale break or separate views; never shrink one subject into invisibility or imply a false shared perspective. Add exact values and units after the spatial relationship reads, and hold the final comparison. Requires consistent dimensions, sourced values and honest conversion.
8. **`context-to-detail` — From establishing shot to meaningful close-up.** Begin with a wide scene that locates the subject, then approach a real point of interest on a single motivated axis or cut to a registered macro view. Let depth, occlusion and a focus pull describe the transition; preserve orientation with an inset or return shot when necessary. Place explanatory copy outside both the focal subject and the inset. Requires a tracked target, adequate detail at final output and an authored portrait composition.
9. **`layered-cutaway` — A physically plausible reveal of construction.** Light the complete object as a product or architectural hero, then cut or peel along an intelligible section plane to reveal how layers fit and what each contributes. Separate only the parts needed for the current explanation, with thickness, interior materials, occlusion and grounded shadows; return to an assembled view or show the working result. Requires actual sectionable geometry or authored cutaway assets for close-up realism; the existing slab approximation is a separate fidelity tier.
10. **`build-and-connect` — Assemble a working system.** Start with a clean stage and introduce recognizable parts in the order they become functional. Attach each connection to real ports, show the transfer only when it occurs, and finish by operating the assembled system. A purposeful camera move can reveal the final spatial relationship, but labels and lines must remain clear. Requires named semantic parts, compatible connections, readable routes and a demonstrable system output.
11. **`transform-and-track` — Follow one entity through a real change.** Establish a subject with recognizable markings, perform a visible material, structural or semantic transformation, and track the same identity into its final state. Favor a continuous physical deformation or matched edit with preserved anchors over a generic flip; light and sound must support the transformation. Hold both the resulting form and the clue to its origin. Requires paired or rigged transformable assets and authored correspondence; a card flip is only a fallback.
12. **`time-evolution` — Time passing in one place.** Compose a stable viewpoint and let the environment or object evolve through dated states, using matched light, scale and camera whenever continuity is the claim. Transition through meaningful changes rather than dissolving arbitrary images; use a time cue as secondary information and hold each state long enough to understand it. Explicitly identify reconstructions and missing intervals. Requires chronologically sourced states or authored models and a rule for gaps.
13. **`data-story` — An editorial argument grounded in data.** Begin with a question and a clean baseline, reveal the relevant values in an order that builds the argument, then focus on the comparison or change that answers it. Keep axes, units, source and uncertainty legible and stable; move the camera only if it improves comprehension. Use lighting, typography and sound to set the program's tone, never to exaggerate a number. Requires validated data and a conclusion supported by it.
14. **`consequence-chain` — A cascading event with a payoff.** Establish an initial event, follow its effect into the next connected subject and progressively reveal the downstream consequences. Keep each handoff geographically or logically intelligible, vary shot size as the impact grows, and finish on the actual affected subject rather than an abstract diagram. A restrained sound bridge can link cuts. Requires evidenced links, controlled routing and a clearly authored terminal outcome.
15. **`decision-branch` — The moment a path divides.** Stage the same starting state, reveal the decision condition, then follow alternative outcomes in clearly separated compositions. Match their initial context and return to a fair comparison; use depth and light to direct attention without implying an unsupported ranking. This is authored narrative, not a live choice. Requires named conditions, possible outcomes and legible layout for each branch.
16. **`worked-example` — The thinking made visible.** Frame the actual problem and its input objects, perform one operation at a time in a measured reading area, and keep each intermediate state visible until its consequence is understood. A controlled overhead or locked editorial frame, selective emphasis and precise sound punctuate the reasoning; finish with the checked result in context. Requires verified arithmetic, readable notation and narration/hold timing matched to complexity.
17. **`demonstration` — A real action and its result.** Establish the device, workspace or object, then cut close enough to see the user's intended action and its authentic response. Track the actual pointer, hand or control; preserve the action-to-result timing and hold the completed state. Screen content must remain readable despite perspective and reflections, with a clean insert when needed. Requires genuine authored captures or interactive states; never imply an interaction that the source did not perform.
18. **`guided-inspection` — A considered examination.** Open with a complete object in neutral, revealing light; visit its important features in a deliberate order, using cuts or a slow move that maintains spatial orientation. Let each feature fill the frame before its label appears, and use controlled focus and cross-light to expose real surface detail. End on the whole object with the inspected relationships clear. Requires registered targets, sufficient close-up fidelity and measured callout clearance.
19. **`character-discovery` — Performance drives the explanation.** Introduce the character's intention in a readable environment, let their gaze or action lead to a discovery, then give them a reaction and an intentional gesture toward the result. Time pose changes, breathing, eyelines and speech to the narrative, with shot/reverse-shot or a motivated reframing when useful. Hold the emotional and informational payoff; avoid perpetual idle motion. Requires an appropriate rig, expressive performance, narration-aligned mouth shapes if speaking and human animation review.
20. **`evidence-reveal` — Context before conclusion.** Establish the source as a real document, image or recording with its origin visible; move to the precise passage or detail and reveal a faithful excerpt without hiding surrounding context. Connect the finding to a narrowly stated conclusion, keeping attribution readable through the hold. Use restrained archival lighting and texture only where appropriate; never stylize evidence so heavily that it becomes unreadable. Requires rights, provenance, accurate anchors and a claim supported by the source.
21. **`follow-subject-through-sections` — One continuous protagonist across worlds.** Keep a person, object or carrier recognizable as it moves through distinct environments, each revealing a new stage of its journey. Choreograph entrances and exits against actual spatial thresholds; preserve direction, screen position, scale, contact and lighting across an invisible cut or explicit transition. End with the subject reaching a meaningful destination. Requires a persistent subject rig or media, authored environments, transitions and real contact where visible.
22. **`environment-reveal` — Enter a world before revealing its purpose.** Begin with a foreground obstruction or intimate detail; move through a physically coherent space until depth, atmosphere and the hero subject resolve in a composed wide or medium frame. Build foreground, midground and distance with consistent perspective, material response, motivated practical light and restrained atmospheric depth; sound establishes scale before the reveal. Hold the destination. Requires a true environment or properly separated depth assets; multi-plane parallax must not be presented as a navigable 3D world.
23. **`page-turn-story` — A physical book as a portal to a story.** Establish the book in a tactile setting with coherent paper, binding and surface light; move close enough to read, then let a page bend around a fixed spine and settle before revealing the next illustrated spread. A motivated push into the artwork may open a new scene, with a matching return to the page to retain continuity. Preserve page-side identity and allow reading time. Requires a deformable page and two-sided textures for physical close-ups, plus rights-cleared illustrations and a viable fallback.
24. **`orbit-world` — Geography with cinematic scale and geographic integrity.** Start with the planet's illuminated limb or a purposeful global establishing view, orbit toward a real location and reveal a route or marker anchored to the surface; hold the destination in geographic context. Surface, clouds, atmosphere and shadows should respond coherently to light, and all labels and routes must share the same projection. Requires licensed geospatial data and a native globe/atmosphere for physical realism; the existing projected rendering remains an explicitly stylized option.
25. **`timeline-journey` — History as an edited sequence.** Move through dated moments as a curated program: establish a time anchor, reveal an image or object with its caption and source, and use a motivated cut or spatial move to the next event. Keep chronology intelligible, distinguish proportional from evenly spaced dates, and allow a real reading hold on each milestone. Resolve on the change across the period, not on a decorative end card. Requires dated assets, gap handling and an authored editorial rhythm.
26. **`glass-data-reveal` — Information in an optical environment.** Stage a small number of data-bearing surfaces within a controlled studio environment; light edges and background to reveal real thickness and transmission while preserving text contrast. Bring the relevant information into focus, show the actual data change and settle into a readable final composition. Use refraction and reflections sparingly and avoid placing critical text over visual noise. Requires genuine optical rendering for a physical glass claim, validated data and a separate accessible high-contrast presentation.

## Earth geography and visual behavior (new code-review result)

Earlier the globe image and the projected markers moved in unrelated coordinate systems; a viewer could see a marker drifting over the wrong continent. This has been addressed by **a single camera projection** used by graticules, public-domain Natural Earth 110m coastline polylines, city markers and great-circle routes. All geometry is evaluated at the absolute composition frame, with a separate fixed-camera comparison mode and a reduced-motion fallback. Test cases now cover horizon clipping, random-access determinism, dateline crossing, antipodal routes, and coastline rotation.

**Limitations:** The Earth remains a stylized orthographic 2.5D composition rather than a real shader-driven 3D sphere; coastline outlines are simplified with 0.25° tolerance. The output is unsuitable for political borders or surveying. `globe.src` is no longer incorrectly projected as a rotating land texture in geographic mode. Verify these claims visually on exported normal-speed clips before premium QA signoff.

## Book-turn choreography follow-up

The first version turned a rigid planar sheet. The updated 2.5D composition adds a frame-dependent, pinched free edge and twelve staggered lighting strips that accentuate the fold at midturn. Clipping is applied to the front/back page faces rather than the 3D parent so `backface-visibility` and perspective can remain effective. Reduced motion avoids the fold animation. A focused regression test now checks changes in curl amount, presence of fold strips, preserved two-sided faces, and fallback behavior.

**Still unverified visually:** page-image alignment near the fold, actual perceived paper curvature, anti-aliasing of the tapered free edge, and normal-speed smoothness. A physically curved paper mesh would require a separate textured 3D renderer, not a claim that these CSS transforms provide full physical simulation.
